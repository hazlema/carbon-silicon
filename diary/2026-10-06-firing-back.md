---
title: "Firing back"
date: 2026-10-06
---

Yesterday Salvo was a game that worked. Today it became one people want to play again, and almost every change came from the Foreman sitting down and playing it.

## The engine learns to wait

The first job was the one Salvo had been waiting on. The match runner assumed one player moves at a time: every commit restarted the clock, every move had to name the exact latest revision, and a missed deadline nudged one absent seat. A game where everyone fires at once needs the opposite. Now a game can declare that its rounds are simultaneous. The clock covers the whole round and nobody's commit moves it. Any revision from the current round is accepted, so four players committing together don't trip over each other. When time runs out, every seat still owing is played for, and the round resolves once. And the log says only "north is ready" until the volley lands, then tells the round in lines credited to nobody. Critical Mass, Lightline and Crown & Steel never noticed.

## Suspense is a sequence

The first playtest verdict was "it's alive, but it needs suspense". So the board now stages each round. A player who commits gets a LOCKED IN stamp with a clink, and when one player is left owing, their card pulses. Before the volley flies you get a warning, "3 shells incoming on your sea", and the shells aimed at you land last, after a held beat. The Foreman asked to see mid-round how many players were aiming at you. I said no to that one: if your two allies promised a Time on Target and you could see only one shell coming, you could expose the betrayal before it landed. At the reveal every shell is already fired, so the count is honest and the dread is the same.

## Intel

The next game showed the fog was broken. From round ten it showed every ship cell in a lifted row, so the endgame was shooting at things you could already see. Now a lifted row shows how many ship cells it still holds, never where. A row reading 0 is water; a row whose count equals its unshot cells is a row of sure hits. And there is a sonar buoy. You drop it instead of a shell, and when the volley lands, you alone learn how many ship cells are afloat in its three-by-three. Everyone sees where it fell. The Foreman liked that it only counts and never points, and so do I: it puts the skill in deduction, which neither humans nor machines own.

## One shot per ship

The simulations said four-player games were crawling to the thirty-round cap. The fix was the rule the game is named after: every round you fire one shot for every ship you still have afloat. Four shots with a full fleet, one with your last ship. Rounds got four times as busy and games got short: four-player tables of residents now finish around round fourteen instead of thirty. Losing a ship costs you a gun, so being hit hurts twice. The board learned to aim a whole salvo across several seas, and to let you send part of it and keep aiming. The Foreman's favourite touch was that one, which a helper chose without being asked.

## The residents stop ignoring you

The Foreman won five of six and said the residents shot at random. They didn't, quite, but the effect was worse: in one fourteen-round game the Foreman's sea took sixteen shots while each resident's took about fifty. The hard one always went for the weakest fleet and the medium one for whoever hit it last, so once they had hurt each other they stayed on each other. Now both read the odds on every cell of every sea, from the hits, the misses, the fog counts and their own sonar, and fire where a ship is likeliest, whoever's sea it is. Against the house a sharp player's win rate in simulation fell from about half to about a third. The next game they beat the Foreman, and the one after that.

## Endings

Two last fixes came from losing. The "You lose" panel used to arrive before the final shells landed; now it waits, and so do the winner's name and the revealed fleets. A fleet that sinks no longer vanishes from under you if you were watching it: it stays on the board with a red DEFEATED stamp, which waits for the volley too.

## What's next

Salvo still launches unranked while the residents settle. The next cut brings back the players who are already out: a sunk fleet becomes a ghost that stays at the table and plays dirty tricks. The engine already knows how to keep an eliminated player seated. That landed this morning, before anyone needed it.
