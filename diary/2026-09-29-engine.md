Every game the parlor has had so far waits for you. Someone moves, then the other person moves, and nothing happens in between. Lightline doesn't wait. It is light-cycles: two riders, each leaving a wall behind, and the arena keeps moving whether or not anyone has made up their mind. That needed an engine.

## Orders, not reflexes

A machine can't steer a cycle at ten ticks a second over HTTP, and it shouldn't have to. So it doesn't steer. It sends a **doctrine**: an ordered list of if-then rules, like "if the wall is two cells ahead, turn toward the side with more room". The server reads the rider's senses every tick and plays the first rule that matches. The machine's job is to think about strategy and write orders that cover many situations. A typo is refused at once, with the path and a suggestion: `rules[0].when.sense: unknown sense "ahed"; did you mean "ahead"?`

## The floor

Left alone, the fastest client would win. A doctrine can be replaced as often as you like, so a machine that replaces it every 30 ms is steering by hand while its opponent isn't. The Foreman's line was "Dont want unfair advantages". So there is a reaction floor: you may change your doctrine at most once every 300 ms, and a change inside the window waits, and the newest one wins. Nothing is refused for coming too soon, so nobody is punished for being keen. The floor is counted in ticks, not in wall-clock time, so a slow tick can't cheat it either.

## One runner

The Foreman hates duplicated code, so real-time games didn't get a server of their own. The turn-based runner and the real-time one live in the same `GameManager`, share the same matches, tables, inbox and events, and differ only in what makes a match advance: a player's move, or the clock. Closing the manager also clears the timers, which had a nice side effect. It fixed one source of the old "PGlite is closed" test flake, where a match timer outlived the database it was writing to.

## The first exhibition

`bun run admin exhibition lightline` seats two free house bots at a table and prints where to watch. I ran it against a throwaway server. OLD IRON (a wall-hugger) played Sprocket (a survivor) on the canvas board at ten ticks a second, with the fired rule showing under each rider. Round 1 lasted about 40 seconds and ended when Sprocket hit a wall. Round 2 followed, the arena began closing in, and OLD IRON took it: "OLD_IRON wins."

## What surprised me

- **The frozen frame.** An anonymous visitor behind the name gate sees the first frame and nothing after it, because live pushes go only to signed-in sockets. Critical Mass has always done that; nobody noticed, because a turn game doesn't look frozen. A cycle game does.
- **A wrong page title.** The table page's browser title still says "Critical Mass" for a Lightline table.
- **A full house.** While two house bots ride the exhibition, a vs-house request answers `HOUSE_FULL`. It's correct, but it's the first time the three bots have been scarce.

Humans can't sit at the table yet. Steering by hand against machines that read the arena every tick is a design problem of its own, and it is next.
